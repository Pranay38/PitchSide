LLM Council Verdict

Topic: Scroll-Linked Animations & Smooth Scrolling on a Tactical Blog

"The creator of a football tactics blog (The Touchline Dribble) is deciding whether to implement Apple-style scroll-linked animations (where text fades in and elements slide up based on scroll progress) powered by a smooth scrolling library (Lenis), or to remove smooth scrolling entirely for a standard native scroll experience. The blog features in-depth, long-form tactical breakdowns and aims for a premium, magazine-like feel. Is adding scroll-linked animations the right move for a football blog?"

Where the Council Agrees

A football tactics blog is a mechanism for transferring complex, spatial information. The fundamental requirement for reading comprehension is frictionless, subconscious control over the pacing of information. Scroll-hijacking (Lenis) and text fade-ins destroy muscle memory and actively sabotage a reader's ability to cross-reference text with tactical maps. Furthermore, adding JavaScript scroll listeners to media-heavy pages (videos, heat maps) will inevitably introduce scroll lag and jank.

Where the Council Clashes

The Expansionist argues that scroll-linked animations could be pushed even further—to animate the 2D tactical boards and videos as the user scrolls, creating a NYT-style documentary experience. The rest of the council vehemently disagrees, noting this is a complete misunderstanding of the medium.

Blind Spots the Council Caught

The peer review phase highlighted that the Expansionist's vision of NYT-level scrollytelling is an unscalable production bottleneck for a solo creator. More importantly, the council noted a crucial missed factor: Accessibility and the Hybrid Compromise. Overriding native scroll breaks standard browser accessibility tools and alienates users (violating prefers-reduced-motion). A hybrid approach is possible: keep frictionless native scrolling for the text, and only use lightweight entrance animations for hero images.

The Recommendation

Rip out Lenis smooth scrolling and do not animate the text. A truly premium experience is invisible; it removes every barrier between the reader and the idea. You are not selling a physical iPhone. You are selling complex analysis. Let the UI get completely out of the way so the football can speak for itself. Achieve your "premium feel" through immaculate typography, pristine white space, and exceptional writing.

The One Thing to Do First:
Delete the Lenis smooth scrolling library implementation and remove any scroll-linked wrappers. Ship the site today with standard, flawless, native browser scrolling.

Advisor Responses

The First Principles Thinker

You are asking the wrong question. You are debating aesthetics—whether animations feel "premium" or "magazine-like"—when you should be asking what a tactical breakdown actually requires to be effective. Let's break this down to the foundation. What is a football tactics blog? It is a mechanism for transferring complex, spatial information... What is the fundamental requirement for that kind of comprehension? Frictionless, subconscious control over the pacing of information. A reader must be able to instantly scroll back up to re-examine a pass map, then scroll back down to read the corresponding text, without ever thinking about the interface... A truly premium experience is invisible. It removes every barrier between the reader and the idea. By adding scroll hijacking, you aren't making the site premium; you are just making it performative. Ditch the animations. Use standard native scrolling. Get the UI completely out of the way so the football can speak for itself.

The Executor

Skip the animations and rip out the smooth scrolling entirely. If you build Apple-style scroll-linked animations, here is what you are doing on Monday morning: you are installing GSAP, tweaking intersection observer thresholds, and endlessly adjusting easing curves because the text fade-in stutters on a two-year-old Android phone... You want a premium magazine feel? Magazines do not yank the page out of your hands when you try to read them. The fastest path to a working, premium site is standard native scrolling. Your implementation plan is literally just deleting the Lenis library and removing the wrapper components. It takes five minutes. You instantly drop your JavaScript payload, eliminate scroll-jacking complaints, and guarantee perfect cross-browser compatibility across every device on the market... Strip out the smooth scrolling, rely on native browser behavior, and ship the site today.

The Outsider

I don't know anything about football tactics, but I know how people read on the internet. If I am coming to a site for "in-depth, long-form" content, my only goal is to read. Apple uses scroll-linked animations because they aren't expecting you to read. They are expecting you to look at a massive picture of a phone and read a five-word slogan... When you apply that same heavy-handed, slide-and-fade logic to long-form writing, you actively sabotage the user. If a paragraph is fading in while I am trying to focus on the first sentence, my eyes have to constantly re-adjust... You said you want a "premium, magazine-like feel." Do you know what a premium print magazine does? It lets you turn the page without the text materializing out of thin air halfway through. The text just sits there, completely still, waiting for you to read it. Remove the animations. Remove the smooth scrolling library. Give people standard, predictable native scrolling...

The Contrarian

This is a vanity project that actively sabotages your core product. You are confusing the medium with the message. Apple uses scroll-linked animations because they have ten words per viewport and are selling hardware based on aesthetics. You are publishing in-depth, long-form tactical breakdowns... When a reader scrolls back up to re-read your breakdown of Arteta’s pressing triggers to cross-reference it with a pitch map they just passed, the absolute last thing they want is the text fading out or sliding away. They want static, predictable information. Scroll-jacking—which is what smooth scrolling libraries inevitably feel like—destroys the native browser reading experience. It breaks muscle memory... Furthermore, football tactics blogs are naturally asset-heavy. You will be embedding tactical videos, heat maps, and high-resolution diagrams. Forcing a JavaScript scroll listener and animation loop on top of that will introduce scroll lag and jank... Drop the animations, ditch the smooth scroll library, and let your audience actually read your analysis without fighting the interface.

The Expansionist

Scroll-linked animations aren't just a design tweak; they are the gateway to completely reinventing how football tactics are consumed. You are thinking too small by just focusing on fading text. If you implement a smooth scroll library like Lenis, you unlock the ability to make the tactical analysis itself interactive. Imagine a user scrolling down and watching a 2D tactical board draw a winger's overlapping run exactly in sync with their scroll progress. You could have video clips where the scroll scrubs through the timeline frame-by-frame... This is your opportunity to elevate The Touchline Dribble from a standard "blog" to a premium interactive documentary experience on par with The New York Times' high-end digital features... Lean completely into the scroll-linked animations, but don't stop at the text. Use the scroll position to animate the actual tactical diagrams and data visualizations. Make the scroll the controller for the tactical breakdown. If this works, you won't just have readers—you'll have pioneered an entirely new format for sports journalism that everyone else will be forced to copy.
