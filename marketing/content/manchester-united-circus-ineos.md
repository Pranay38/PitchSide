# The Circus: Everything Wrong With Manchester United Since INEOS Took Over

There's a moment in every institutional decline where the incompetence stops being tragic and starts being performance art. In psychology, it's called the "escalation of commitment"—the irrational drive to double down on failing strategies because admitting defeat would mean acknowledging the massive cost of previous failures.

Manchester United passed that threshold somewhere around the time they sacked their sporting director after five months, fired two managers in 14 months, and spent over half a billion pounds to finish fifteenth in the Premier League.

Welcome to the circus. Admission is £60 a matchday. The clowns are in the boardroom.

When Sir Jim Ratcliffe completed his 27.7% minority stake acquisition in February 2024 and assumed control of football operations, the promise was simple: professionalize the club, bring in world-class football people, and end the post-Ferguson rot that had been festering since 2013. Two and a half years later, Manchester United have burned through two permanent managers, one interim, one sporting director, approximately £550 million in transfer spending, and the patience of an entire fanbase.

**And the Glazers are still in charge.**

> **Key Takeaways**
> - INEOS have spent approximately £550 million on transfers since taking over in February 2024, cycling through two permanent managers and one interim while the team finished 15th in 2024-25 — their lowest league position since the 1989-90 season
> - The summer 2026 transfer window drew widespread fan fury: £152 million spent entirely on midfielders while the left-back position and striker role went unaddressed, earning a 2-4/10 rating from supporters
> - INEOS cut approximately 250 staff in 2024 and another 150-200 in 2025, removed free staff meals to save £1 million per year, yet continued spending over £100 million per transfer window on players
> - Sporting director Dan Ashworth was hired from Newcastle, then sacked after just five months in December 2024 over "disagreements" about the club's direction
> - Fan group "The 1958" has organized continuous protests against both the Glazers and INEOS, with sentiment shifting from viewing Ratcliffe as a saviour to seeing him as "complicit" in the decline

## The INEOS Takeover: A Timeline of Chaos

Let's start from the beginning, because the speed at which this project has unravelled is genuinely breathtaking.

**February 2024** — Ratcliffe's deal closes. He takes a 27.7% minority stake but, crucially, gains full operational control of football matters. The Glazer family retains majority voting rights. The press conference is optimistic. Words like "world-class" and "cultural reset" get deployed. Everyone smiles.

**Summer 2024** — The first transfer window. INEOS spend approximately £200 million on five major signings: Leny Yoro (£52M from Lille), Manuel Ugarte (£50.5M from PSG), Matthijs de Ligt and Noussair Mazraoui in a £60M double deal from Bayern Munich, and Joshua Zirkzee (£36.5M from Bologna). The signings look reasonable on paper. Then Yoro breaks his foot in preseason. Zirkzee can't score from open play. De Ligt and Mazraoui are functional but not transformative. Ugarte is good but plays in a system that changes every other week.

**October 2024** — Erik ten Hag is finally sacked. The writing had been on the wall since the club reportedly offered the job to Thomas Tuchel during the summer *while ten Hag was still manager*. Ruud van Nistelrooy takes charge as interim.

**November 2024** — Rúben Amorim is appointed head coach. He arrives from Sporting CP with a reputation for tactical innovation and a non-negotiable 3-4-3 system. The vibes are immaculate. For about six weeks.

**December 2024** — Dan Ashworth, the sporting director INEOS poached from Newcastle United (after a protracted legal dispute), is sacked after barely five months in the job. The official line: "mutual agreement." The reality: INEOS became dissatisfied with his work, deeming his footballing perspective "too UK-focused" and criticizing his presentations and recommendations.

Five months. They fought a legal battle with Newcastle to hire a man they fired five months later. You cannot make this up.

If you're wondering how a club can lose its tactical identity this quickly, we broke down the systemic problem in our analysis of [why Madrid's tactical collapse isn't on Mbappé](/stories/the-vini-jr-problem-why-madrids-tactical-collapse-isnt-on-mbappe) — the same dysfunction applies at United, just with less talent to paper over the cracks.

## 15th Place: The Lowest Point in 35 Years

The 2024-25 Premier League season under Amorim was nothing short of catastrophic.

Manchester United finished **15th** with just **42 points** — their lowest league position since the 1989-90 season and their fewest points in a top-flight campaign since they were literally relegated in 1973-74. Let that sink in. The most successful club in English football history came closer to a relegation battle than a title challenge.

*For more on how elite clubs lose their competitive edge while others pass them by, see our tactical deep dive on [the Vini Jr problem at Real Madrid](/stories/the-vini-jr-problem-why-madrids-tactical-collapse-isnt-on-mbappe).*

Amorim's stubborn commitment to his 3-4-3 formation — deployed in 45 of his 47 Premier League matches — exposed a squad that simply did not have the personnel to execute it. The wing-back positions demanded players United didn't possess. The centre-back trio required a level of coordination this squad couldn't deliver. And Amorim refused to adapt.

His overall Premier League win percentage: **31.9%**. Over 47 matches. At Manchester United.

The one silver lining — and "silver" is doing heavy lifting here — was the run to the Europa League final in May 2025. But even that felt like an accident of the draw more than proof of progress. And it disguised just how broken the domestic campaign truly was.

## The Revolving Door of Decision-Makers

If there's a single image that captures the INEOS era, it's the boardroom whiteboard being erased and rewritten every few months.

Here's the body count so far:

### Managers Burned Through

| Manager | Appointed | Sacked | Duration | Notes |
|---------|-----------|--------|----------|-------|
| Erik ten Hag | Already in post | October 2024 | ~5 months under INEOS | Offered a contract extension in the summer then sacked months later |
| Ruud van Nistelrooy | October 2024 | November 2024 | ~3 weeks | Interim only |
| Rúben Amorim | November 2024 | Early January 2026 | 14 months | 63 games: 25W, 15D, 23L. Sacked after "disagreement with hierarchy" |
| Darren Fletcher | January 5, 2026 | January 13, 2026 | 8 days | Interim only |
| Michael Carrick | January 13, 2026 | Present | ~8 months | Finished 3rd in 2025-26. Currently in charge |

Five different men have managed this football club in the space of two and a half years. Five. Even by post-Ferguson Manchester United standards, this is absurd. Chelsea are looking at this and thinking "steady on, lads."

### The Executive Circus

The off-pitch churn is equally damaging:

- **Dan Ashworth** (Sporting Director): Hired from Newcastle after legal dispute. Sacked after 5 months (December 2024)
- **Omar Berrada** (CEO): Poached from Manchester City. Still in post but operating in increasingly constrained conditions
- **Jason Wilcox** (Technical Director): Remains in post but reportedly working under constantly shifting strategic priorities
- **Jim Ratcliffe**: The man at the top, who has been accused by fans and pundits of being an absentee owner who treats football like a spreadsheet exercise

Philosopher Albert Camus wrote of Sisyphus, cursed to push a boulder up a mountain for eternity, only to watch it roll back down. United's board are pushing a £500 million boulder, but instead of the mountain, they're pushing it through a revolving door.

The most damning stat isn't on the pitch. It's this: INEOS hired a sporting director, fought a legal battle for months to get him, and then sacked him in less time than it takes most people to finish a Netflix series.

## £550 Million for... What Exactly?

Let's talk money. Because the truly staggering part of this story isn't just the dysfunction — it's the cost of the dysfunction.

Behavioral economists call it the "Sunk Cost Fallacy." When clubs treat underperforming players as intangible assets whose value must be justified through playing time, they ignore the *opportunity cost*. You can't fix the team because you're too busy justifying the price tag of the players breaking it. INEOS promised to be the antidote to this logic. Instead, they weaponized it.

### Summer 2024: The "Reset" Window (~£200M)

The marquee window. The "new Manchester United." INEOS brought in five major signings — Yoro, de Ligt, Mazraoui, Ugarte, and Zirkzee — and the narrative was that these were "profile" signings based on data and scouting rather than name recognition. Smart, right?

Except Yoro broke his foot immediately. Zirkzee looked like a man who'd been told there was a football pitch nearby and accidentally wandered onto it. De Ligt was fine. Mazraoui was fine. Ugarte was good but couldn't compensate for a midfield that was being dismantled around him.

Net result: 15th in the league. The most expensive 15th-place finish in the history of the sport.

### Summer 2025: The Attack Overhaul (~£210M)

With the attack clearly dysfunctional, INEOS went big — £210 million on three forwards: Benjamin Šeško from RB Leipzig (£66-73M), Bryan Mbeumo from Brentford (£70M), and Matheus Cunha from Wolves (£62.5M). These were supposedly the first phase of a "three-tier squad rebuild" aiming for title contention by 2028.

Here's the problem: you don't fix a house by building a beautiful kitchen while the foundation is crumbling. The attack improved, sure. Šeško and Mbeumo finished as joint top scorers. But the defence was still leaking goals, the midfield was still overrun, and the system changed every time a new manager walked through the door.

### Summer 2026: The Circus Reaches Its Peak (~£152M)

And this is where the fans finally lost it.

After two windows focused on defence and attack, the 2026 window was supposed to address the midfield AND the left-back position. What did United actually do?

**They spent £152 million — entirely on midfielders:**

| Player | From | Fee |
|--------|------|-----|
| Carlos Baleba | Brighton | £65M + £5M add-ons |
| Andrey Santos | Chelsea | £48M + £2M add-ons |
| Youri Tielemans | Aston Villa | £35M |
| Tynan Thompson | Tottenham | £4M + £4M add-ons |
| Karl Darlow | Leeds | Free transfer |

That's it. No left-back. No striker backup. No centre-back depth. They failed to sign Rayan Ait-Nouri from Manchester City on deadline day after the clubs couldn't agree on a loan-to-buy structure. Luke Shaw's hamstrings have been on a permanent vacation for three years, and INEOS' solution was to cross their fingers and hope this is the season he stays fit.

As one exasperated fan on Reddit summarized the strategy: *"How about we rebuild what the squad needs when it needs it, not a year or five years later? They are leaving the team dangerously thin for a season that includes Champions League football."*

<!-- [UNIQUE INSIGHT] -->

Meanwhile, **André Onana was loaned out to Trabzonspor**. The goalkeeper they spent £47 million on two years ago. Loaned to Trabzonspor. In Turkey. And **Rasmus Højlund** — their supposed long-term striker investment — was sold to Napoli.

The dressing room reportedly wanted more. Pundits were stunned. Jamie O'Hara called the lack of defensive backing for Michael Carrick an absolute "disgrace." Gary Neville publicly expressed "amazement" that the club did not prioritise the backline, warning of a "real problem" heading into their Champions League campaign against Bayern Munich and Atletico Madrid. Fans rated the window between 2/10 and 4/10. Rio Ferdinand pointed out that the club has over £400 million in outstanding transfer fee payments from previous windows, which was strangling the current budget.

**Translation: INEOS spent over half a billion pounds in two years, then couldn't afford to fix the problems that half a billion pounds created.**

## The Cost-Cutting Hypocrisy

If the transfer dysfunction wasn't enough, the off-pitch optics have been equally appalling.

In July 2024, INEOS announced a "transformation plan" that made approximately **250 staff** redundant. In February 2025, another **150-200 jobs** were cut. Dedicated, long-serving club employees — the people who make matchdays possible — were made redundant in the name of "efficiency."

And then there's the story that perfectly encapsulates the INEOS culture: they **removed free hot meals** for non-playing staff. The staff canteen at Old Trafford was shut down. At Carrington, non-playing staff were offered **soup and bread** while the players continued eating gourmet. The projected saving? **£1 million per year**.

One million pounds. To a club that had just spent £200 million on transfers in a single window.

Think about that. They are removing hot lunches from the people who park the cars and maintain the pitch while simultaneously spending £65 million on a midfielder from Brighton.

This isn't fiscal responsibility. This is a corporation cosplaying as a football club.

## The Fans Have Had Enough

The fan reaction has evolved through distinct stages:

**Stage 1: Hope (February-August 2024)**
When Ratcliffe arrived, the dominant emotion was relief. After years of Glazer neglect, any change felt like progress. Social media was cautiously optimistic. The 1958 fan group paused their protests.

**Stage 2: Concern (September 2024-May 2025)**
As results cratered and the 15th-place finish materialised, concern replaced hope. The Ashworth sacking raised eyebrows. The ten Hag extension-then-sacking looked amateurish. But there was still a willingness to give INEOS time.

**Stage 3: Fury (June 2025-Present)**
The 2026 summer window was the breaking point. The cynical club refrain of *"Judge us at the end of the window"* was thrown back in their faces by supporters after the deadline passed with glaring holes unaddressed. Fan group "The 1958" resumed protests against both the Glazers AND INEOS — a significant shift. Where Ratcliffe was initially seen as the saviour, he is now viewed as "complicit" in the decline.

The key driver of the current fury isn't just results — it's the disconnect. Fans see staff being cut to save £1M while £65M is spent on Carlos Baleba. They see a 100,000-seater stadium proposal (that critics have mockingly called a "circus tent") while the current squad can't defend a set piece. They see INEOS promising professionalism while cycling through managers faster than a Fantasy Football player.

Roy Keane, never one to sugarcoat, has been relentless in his criticism on ITV and the *Stick to Football* podcast, hammering the club's transfer business and defensive vulnerabilities. Neville has called the defensive neglect "amazingly risky" ahead of Champions League fixtures against Bayern and Atletico.

## The Michael Carrick Effect — And Its Limits

There is one bright spot in this entire mess, and it wears a quiet demeanour and a Middlesbrough coat.

Michael Carrick took charge on January 13, 2026, after Amorim's dismissal and an eight-day Darren Fletcher interim stint. He inherited a squad sitting 6th in the table and somehow dragged it to a **3rd-place finish** — securing Champions League football and providing the only genuine reason for optimism in the entire INEOS era.

But here's the uncomfortable truth: Carrick achieved this with essentially the same squad Amorim couldn't get above 6th. The difference wasn't talent; it was tactical pragmatism. Where Amorim insisted on his 3-4-3 regardless of personnel, Carrick adapted. Where Amorim clashed with the hierarchy, Carrick kept his head down and coached.

The problem is that Carrick's excellent rescue job is now being used as justification for the 2026 window's inadequacy. The logic from the boardroom appears to be: "Carrick got 3rd with this squad, why does he need a left-back?" As if nearly half a season of overperformance is somehow a sustainable model rather than a ticking time bomb.

The squad is still thin. The defence is still fragile. Luke Shaw is still made of glass. And INEOS have essentially told Carrick to win the Champions League with a midfield and prayers.

## Everyone Else Moved Forward. United Stood Still.

While Manchester United have been busy setting fire to half a billion pounds and calling it a "project," look at what happened around them:

- **Arsenal** became genuine, sustained title contenders
- **Liverpool** seamlessly transitioned from Klopp to a new era
- **Manchester City** spent £458 million in the summer 2026 window alone and continued to evolve their squad
- **Chelsea** — yes, Chelsea, the other club everyone mocks for chaotic spending — somehow look more coherent than United
- **Newcastle** rebuilt intelligently after losing the sporting director that United sacked five months later

United are trapped in a cycle where they spend enormous sums on players who are either bought for a system that gets abandoned when the manager is sacked, or bought for a manager who gets sacked before the system can be implemented. It's a transfer hamster wheel powered by executive turnover.

## The Fundamental Problem Nobody Wants to Say Out Loud

**The Glazers still own Manchester United.**

Meanwhile, the actual ownership structure — the thing that has been bleeding this club for 20 years — remains untouched.

INEOS are rearranging deck chairs on a ship that the Glazers are still steering into the iceberg. And the deck chairs cost £65 million each.

## What Happens Next?
